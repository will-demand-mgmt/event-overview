AI-Driven Event Management System

A multi-agent assistant layer for the teams that run a live event — one agent per function, one orchestrator watching the seams between them, and a human always holding the cue to say go.

CUE 01

The problem

A TEDx-style event — and most volunteer- or community-run conferences — is coordinated by a handful of team leads, each fluent in their own stream but working mostly blind to what the other streams are deciding in real time. Nobody is negligent; there is simply no shared, running picture of the whole event.

01

No shared state

Curation, Production, Operations, Fundraising, Communications and HR each keep their own version of the plan, updated on their own schedule.

02

Conflicts surface late

A change one team makes quietly breaks another team's plan — and it's usually caught in a spreadsheet update, a missed thread, or the week-of walkthrough.

03

Coordination is unpaid time

Most of this is run by volunteers. Every hour spent reconciling schedules is an hour not spent on the actual event.

CUE 02

How it works

Three layers, deliberately low on autonomy: an assistant per team, a watcher across teams, and a rulebook that both of them have to answer to.

Team agentsone per stream

Each team lead gets a dedicated assistant — closer to a secretary than a decision-maker. It drafts, tracks deadlines, keeps a running log of what changed, and checks outgoing drafts against the guidelines before they go out. It never acts outside its own team's lane.

Orchestratorcross-team watcher

Reads what every agent reports and looks for impact across streams — a schedule change that collides with someone else's booking, a budget move that affects a sponsor deliverable. It only detects and flags. Execution happens after a human validates the call.

Policy enginethe organization's rulebook

Ingests the organization's own guidelines — for this pilot, TED's own Organizer Guide and published event guidelines — and checks every flagged decision and every agent draft against them, so nothing the system proposes drifts outside what's allowed.

Team boundaries here follow TED's own Organizer Guide — Curation, Production, Event Operations, Fundraising & Budget, Communications & Marketing — with one deliberate addition: HR & Volunteers isn't a standalone category in TED's guide, but recruiting, screening and day-of volunteer coordination are substantial enough work, drawn from direct experience running TEDx Torino, to warrant their own agent rather than folding into Operations.

CUE 03

Why now, why this shape

Off-the-shelf event platforms are increasingly bolting AI copilots onto their existing workflows. That's not the gap this explores.

Who's underserved

Volunteer & community-run events

Enterprise event platforms are built for paid ops teams with budget. Community conferences like TEDx franchises rarely have either — this targets exactly that gap, not corporate event teams.

Why per-function agents

Matches how the org already works

Team leads already own a stream end to end. An assistant scoped to their stream fits how the work is actually organized, instead of forcing everyone into one shared tool.

Why a human validator

Cheap detection, careful execution

Letting agents flag conflicts around the clock is low-risk. Letting them execute unsupervised, days before a live event, is not — so execution stays gated on a person.

Why a policy engine

Portable beyond TEDx

Any franchised or brand-governed event has its own rulebook. TED's guidelines are simply the first one loaded in — the mechanism generalizes to any organization's rules.

These are the bets this project is meant to test, not settled conclusions — TEDx Torino is the pilot precisely because there's enough first-hand operating experience to tell quickly whether they hold up.

Me: Come Orchestratore gestirei un conflitto ipotetico tra il team Production e il team Sponsorship? Orchestrator: […] 1. Raccolgo i dati dei due team. 2. Normalizzo le entità e i vincoli. 3. Confronto i vincoli per individuare violazioni. 4. Genero un flag strutturato e lo notifico al validatore con proposte di mitigazione. 5. Il policy engine verifica che la decisione finale sia conforme alle regole TED e a quelle contrattuali. 6. Emetto i comandi di attuazione per i team, mantenendo un log completo. In questo modo il conflitto viene evidenziato prima che le attività operative vengano avviate, garantendo che il rispetto delle linee guida TED e degli accordi rimanga centrale, mentre la decisione finale resta sotto il controllo umano.
